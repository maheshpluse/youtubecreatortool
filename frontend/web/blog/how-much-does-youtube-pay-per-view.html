How Much Does YouTube Pay Per View? The Real Numbers Explained

By The VidSEOKit Editorial Team· September 19, 2026· 10 min read· 2,179 words

YouTube doesn't pay per view — it shares ad revenue. Learn what one view is really worth, how RPM works, Shorts vs long-form, and how to find your own rate.

Advertisement

"How much does YouTube pay per view?" sounds like a question with a single answer. It is not, and anyone who gives you one fixed number — a tenth of a cent, a quarter of a cent, one cent — is quoting an average that may have nothing to do with your channel. Two channels with identical view counts can earn amounts that differ by a factor of ten or more.

This guide explains how YouTube actually pays, why "per view" is the wrong unit, what realistic ranges look like for long-form videos and Shorts, and how to work out a per-view figure for your own channel that you can plan around. Every mechanism described here is taken from YouTube's own documentation, which is linked throughout so you can check it.

The short answer

For monetized long-form videos, many channels earn somewhere between roughly $0.001 and $0.01 per view — that is, $1 to $10 per 1,000 views. Channels in finance, business software and similar high-value niches with audiences in high-spending advertising markets can exceed that range. Entertainment, gaming and music channels with global audiences often sit below it. Shorts typically earn a small fraction of long-form per view.

Those ranges are observations, not guarantees. The rest of this article explains how to replace them with your own number.

Before you can earn anything

Views only earn ad revenue once your channel is accepted into the YouTube Partner Program and you have turned on ads. Views on videos uploaded before you joined can start earning once monetization is enabled on them. Our monetization requirements guide covers the thresholds.

Why YouTube does not pay per view

YouTube does not have a price per view. It shares advertising revenue. According to the YouTube partner earnings overview, creators receive 55% of the net revenue from ads shown on or alongside their long-form videos. For Shorts, ad revenue is pooled and creators receive 45% of their allocated share.

That means a view only produces income if an ad is actually shown — and paid for — during it. Several things prevent that:

  • Not every playback is shown an ad. YouTube controls ad load and does not serve an ad on every view.
  • Viewers using ad blockers see no ads.
  • YouTube Premium members see no ads, though creators receive a share of Premium subscription revenue based on how much those members watch their content.
  • Videos that are not suitable for all advertisers (the yellow icon in Studio) receive limited or no ads.
  • Many advertisers pay only when a viewer watches a certain amount of the ad or clicks it, not simply when it appears.

Divide the income by all views and you get an average per-view figure — but it is an output of those factors, not a rate anyone agreed to pay.

RPM: the number that turns views into money

The number that answers "how much per view" is RPM, revenue per mille. YouTube defines it as your total revenue divided by total views, multiplied by 1,000. It includes all revenue sources YouTube reports in Studio — ads, Premium, channel memberships, Super Chat and others — and it is measured after YouTube's share.

Divide your RPM by 1,000 and you have your real pay per view. An RPM of $4.20 means each view is worth $0.0042 on average.

Do not confuse RPM with CPM. CPM is what advertisers pay per thousand ad impressions, counted only on monetized playbacks and before YouTube's share. CPM is always higher than RPM, often by a factor of two to four. Planning with CPM is the most common reason creators overestimate their earnings. Our guide to what YouTube pays per 1,000 views explains the gap in detail.

The six factors that move your pay per view

1. Audience country

Advertisers bid more to reach viewers in markets with higher consumer spending. The same video watched mainly in the United States, Australia or Western Europe typically earns several times more per view than one watched mainly in lower-spend ad markets. Use our RPM by country tool to see estimated ranges for your audience mix.

2. Niche and advertiser demand

Advertisers pay for audiences likely to buy expensive things. A video about choosing a credit card or business accounting software attracts bids from companies whose customers are worth hundreds of dollars. A comedy sketch attracts broader, cheaper brand advertising. The table below shows broad typical ranges.

3. Video length and mid-roll ads

Videos of eight minutes or longer can carry mid-roll ads, which increases the number of ad opportunities per view. More ad slots do not automatically mean more money — too many interruptions can reduce watch time — but well-placed mid-rolls at natural breaks usually raise RPM on long videos. YouTube's help page on managing mid-roll ad breaks explains the options.

4. Season

Advertising budgets peak in the fourth quarter, before the holiday season, and fall sharply in January. The same channel can see per-view earnings swing substantially between December and January with no change in content.

5. Advertiser-friendliness

Videos that follow the advertiser-friendly content guidelines are eligible for the full range of ads. Strong language in the opening seconds, graphic content, or sensitive topics treated without context can limit ads and reduce per-view earnings on that video.

6. Audience age and device

Older audiences with higher disposable income, and viewers watching on TV screens, often attract higher-value ads. You cannot change these directly, but they explain why two similar channels can differ.

Shorts versus long-form per view

Shorts are monetized differently. Ads run between videos in the Shorts feed rather than inside a single video. YouTube pools that revenue each month, uses part of it to cover music licensing, and allocates the remaining creator pool based on each eligible creator's share of Shorts views. Creators keep 45% of their allocation. The full mechanism is described in YouTube's Shorts monetization policies.

The practical result is that Shorts earn far less per view than long-form. Many channels report Shorts RPMs well below $0.10 per 1,000 views, though this varies. A million Shorts views and a million long-form views are not comparable income.

That does not make Shorts a waste of time. Judge them by what they lead to: new subscribers and viewers who go on to watch long-form videos. If you want to plan a mixed strategy, our Shorts monetization guide covers the trade-offs.

How to find your own per-view rate

If your channel is already monetized, you do not need estimates. You can read your actual number in YouTube Studio:

  1. Open YouTube Studio and go to Analytics.
  2. Choose the Revenue tab.
  3. Set the date range to the last 90 days or more — shorter windows are distorted by seasonality and single videos.
  4. Read the RPM figure. Divide by 1,000 for your per-view average.
  5. Click See more and break RPM down by content type (videos, Shorts, live) and by individual video.

The per-video breakdown is where the useful insight lives. You will usually find that a handful of topics earn two or three times more per view than the rest. Those topics tell you where to focus.

If you are not yet monetized, find three to five monetized channels in your niche with audiences like yours and use the calculator's niche setting as a starting point. Treat the result as a range and update it with your own data as soon as you have it.

Raising what each view is worth

You cannot set your per-view rate, but you can influence it.

  • Choose topics with commercial intent. Videos that help a viewer make a purchase or business decision attract higher-value advertisers. The keyword tool helps you find those topics within your niche, and our list of high-CPC keywords by niche gives starting points.
  • Make longer videos when the topic genuinely needs it. Crossing eight minutes enables mid-rolls, but padding a thin video lowers retention and hurts discovery. Length should follow substance.
  • Keep the opening advertiser-friendly. Most monetization reviews focus heavily on the start of a video, its title and its thumbnail.
  • Improve retention. More watch time means more ad opportunities per view. Structure your video with a strong hook and clear sections — the script generator can help you outline one.
  • Add income that does not depend on ads. Affiliate links, sponsorships and memberships often earn more per view than ads. See our guide to passive income streams for creators.

How YouTube actually sends the money

Earnings accumulate in the AdSense account linked to your channel. They are finalised early in the following month, and a payment is issued once your balance reaches the payment threshold for your currency, provided your identity, address, payment method and tax information have been verified. Google's help page on the AdSense payment timeline describes the schedule.

Tax is withheld or reported according to your location and the tax forms you submit. Creators outside the United States may have US withholding applied to earnings from US viewers unless a tax treaty benefit is claimed. Our guide to YouTube taxes covers the basics, but a local tax professional should confirm your obligations.

Common myths about pay per view

  • "YouTube pays $1 per 1,000 views, full stop." This is a rough floor some channels see, not a rate. Many earn much more, and some earn less.
  • "More subscribers means more pay per view." Subscribers do not directly change the rate. Audience composition and topic do.
  • "Views from my own replays count." YouTube filters out views and ad interactions it considers invalid. Clicking your own ads or asking others to do so violates AdSense policies and can lead to your account being disabled.
  • "Viral videos are the best earners." Viral videos often reach broad, lower-value audiences. Steady evergreen videos with search traffic frequently earn more over time.

The single most useful habit is to stop thinking in "views" and start thinking in "RPM by topic." Once you know which subjects earn the most per view for your audience, you can plan a content calendar that earns more from the same effort.

Other ways YouTube pays that are not "per view"

Once you are in the Partner Program, several features earn money that has nothing to do with ad views. Channel memberships, Super Chat, Super Stickers and Super Thanks let viewers pay you directly. YouTube keeps a share of each, and the remainder appears in your Studio revenue. These sources raise your RPM because RPM counts all revenue divided by all views. A channel with a small but loyal audience can have a far higher RPM than its ad rates alone would suggest.

Outside YouTube's systems, sponsorships and affiliate links are usually the largest earners for mid-sized channels. They are paid per video or per sale rather than per view, but it helps to express them per 1,000 views so you can compare. Our guide on calculating a sponsor rate per 1,000 views shows how.

Frequently asked questions

How much does YouTube pay for one view?

Many monetized long-form channels earn roughly $0.001 to $0.01 per view ($1–$10 RPM), but it varies widely with niche, audience country and season.

Does every view earn money?

No. Only views where an ad is shown and paid for earn ad revenue. Premium views earn a share of subscription revenue.

Do Shorts pay the same as long videos?

No. Shorts revenue is pooled and shared at 45% after music costs, so per-view earnings are usually far lower than long-form.
