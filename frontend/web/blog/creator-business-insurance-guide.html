Insurance for Content Creators: What You Actually Need and When

By The VidSEOKit Editorial Team· October 27, 2025· 6 min read· 1,218 words

Your home policy almost certainly excludes business use, which is discovered at the worst possible moment. The policies that apply to creator work, when each becomes worth buying, and why claims get declined.

Advertisement

Insurance is the least interesting thing a creator can spend an afternoon on, and one of the few that genuinely protects everything else. Most creators never think about it until something happens — a laptop stolen on a shoot, a claim over a review, an injury at a location, a sponsor asking for a certificate before they will sign.

This guide explains which policies actually apply to creator work, what each one covers, and how to think about whether you need it yet. It is educational; policy terms vary enormously and a broker in your own country is the right person to confirm specifics.

Why creators are unusually exposed

A creator business combines risks that normally sit in separate industries. You are simultaneously a publisher, a small manufacturer of media, an equipment owner, sometimes an employer, and increasingly a person with a public profile.

That produces a specific list of things that go wrong: expensive portable equipment that lives in bags and cars; public statements about products and people; filming in places where members of the public can be injured; contractors handling your footage; and a single revenue source that stops entirely if you are ill for three months.

None of these are exotic. They are ordinary business risks that creators tend to carry personally without noticing.

The policies that actually matter

Public liabilityInjury or property damage to third parties caused by your activityFilming outside your home, or any location work

For most creators, the first three are the meaningful ones. Media liability is the one almost nobody has and the one most specific to this work.

Why your home policy will not cover it

This is the assumption that costs people the most, and it is worth being blunt about.

Home and contents policies almost universally exclude business use. The moment your camera is used to earn income, a claim for its theft can be declined on that basis alone — and insurers do ask. Creators discover this at exactly the wrong moment, holding a police report and an empty bag.

The same logic applies to liability. If someone trips over your light stand while you are filming a sponsored video, that is a business activity happening at your home, and a domestic policy is unlikely to respond.

Worth ten minutes today

Read the business-use exclusion in your existing home policy. Not the summary — the actual wording. Most creators are surprised by how broadly it is drafted.

When a sponsor demands a certificate

Beyond a certain size, brand contracts routinely require the creator to hold insurance and to provide evidence of it, sometimes naming the brand as an additional insured party.

This catches creators mid-negotiation. A deal is agreed, the contract arrives, and there is a clause requiring cover the creator does not have and cannot arrange in the two days before the campaign starts. The deal either collapses or is signed without the cover, which means signing a contractual promise you have not kept.

If you are approaching the size where brand deals happen, arranging basic cover in advance turns a potential deal-breaker into a document you email.

When to buy what

A reasonable sequence, rather than buying everything at once:

  1. While it is a hobby with modest income: usually nothing beyond checking whether your gear can be added to an existing policy as specified items with business use declared.
  2. Once you film outside your home: public liability. This is often inexpensive and it is the one venues and councils ask for.
  3. Once your gear is genuinely valuable: a dedicated equipment policy covering worldwide use, theft from vehicles, and accidental damage.
  4. Once you review products or discuss named people: media liability. This is the policy that responds to the claims most specific to publishing.
  5. Once you sell advice, courses or coaching: professional indemnity.
  6. Once the channel is your household income: income protection, which is about you rather than the business.

A better first call than a comparison site

Speak to a broker who handles media, film or creative businesses rather than a general small-business line. Creator work sits awkwardly in standard categories, and a specialist will place it correctly the first time.

Making sure a claim actually pays

Buying a policy is the easy half. Claims are declined for boring, avoidable reasons:

  • Undeclared business use. The single most common. Declare it, even though it costs more.
  • No proof of ownership. Keep purchase receipts and serial numbers in one place, backed up off your laptop — because the laptop may be what was stolen.
  • Unattended vehicle exclusions. Many equipment policies severely limit theft from cars, particularly overnight or when the gear was visible. Read this clause specifically.
  • Territory limits. A domestic policy may not cover a shoot abroad. Check before travelling, not afterwards.
  • Late notification. Policies impose deadlines for reporting incidents. Report early even if you are unsure whether you will claim.
  • Sub-limits per item. A policy with a large total limit may cap any single item well below the value of your main camera.

The practical habit that supports all of this: keep an equipment register — item, serial number, purchase date, price, receipt — and update it whenever you buy something. It takes two minutes per purchase and it is the difference between a claim that pays and a conversation about what you can prove.

Insurance will never make you money and you will resent paying for it in every year that nothing happens. That is precisely what you are buying: the years where nothing happens, and the one where something does.

Important

General educational information only, not insurance advice. Policy names, coverage and availability differ by country and insurer. Speak to a licensed broker about your specific circumstances.

Frequently asked questions

Does my home contents policy cover my camera?

Usually not, once it is used to earn income. Business-use exclusions are standard in home and contents policies, and a claim for stolen equipment can be declined on that basis. Declare business use or arrange a separate policy.

What insurance do brands ask creators for?

Public liability is the most common requirement, sometimes with the brand named as an additional insured party. Larger campaigns may also ask for media liability. Arranging basic cover in advance avoids a deal stalling over paperwork.

What is media liability insurance?

Cover for claims arising from what you publish — defamation, copyright allegations, privacy claims. It is the policy most specific to creator work and the one fewest creators hold.
