The Community Tab: Reaching Your Audience Without Making a Video

By The VidSEOKit Editorial Team· July 17, 2025· 6 min read· 1,224 words

The only place you can reach subscribers without publishing. What the tab is genuinely good at, why polls are the most underrated tool on the platform, and how to support an upload properly.

Advertisement

The Community tab is the only place on YouTube where you can reach your audience without making a video. It costs minutes rather than hours, it appears in subscribers' feeds, and most channels use it to post a thumbnail with the word "new video" and nothing else.

This guide covers what the tab is actually good at, the post types that work, and how to use it to support uploads rather than just announce them.

What this guide covers

What it is genuinely for

Three jobs, none of which is "announce videos".

Staying present between uploads. If you publish fortnightly, your audience hears nothing from you for thirteen days. A channel that appears in the feed twice a week stays familiar, and familiarity drives the click when a video does arrive.

Learning what your audience wants. A poll gives you a direct answer in hours, from the exact people who watch you. This is better market research than any keyword tool, because it comes from your actual audience rather than a general population.

Warming up an upload. Early engagement matters, and a video published to an audience already anticipating it gets a meaningfully better start.

Post types and what each does

TypeEffortBest for
PollUnder a minuteDeciding topics, settling debates, research
ImageLowBehind the scenes, work in progress, results
TextLowA thought, a question, a short useful tip
Video or clipModerateQuick answers that do not justify a full upload
Video shareMinimalResurfacing older work in a relevant context

The pattern worth noticing: the highest-value posts are the cheapest ones. A poll takes under a minute and produces genuinely useful information. A carefully designed announcement graphic takes an hour and produces a click a link would have produced anyway.

Polls are the most underrated tool

Polls get disproportionate engagement because voting is nearly frictionless — one tap, no comment to compose, no exposure. That low barrier means you hear from viewers who never comment, which is most of them.

Ways to use them that actually produce value:

  • Choosing between planned videos. You were going to make one of them anyway; now you know which, and the winners arrive with an audience already invested in the answer.
  • Testing a thumbnail concept. Not a substitute for proper testing, but a fast read on whether an idea is legible.
  • Understanding your audience's level. "Are you just starting or have you been doing this for years?" changes how you should pitch everything.
  • Settling a question the video raised. Extends a video's life and gives people a reason to return.
  • Finding the objection. "What stops you doing this?" surfaces the exact thing your next video should address.

One caution: only ask questions where you will genuinely act on the answer. An audience that votes and sees nothing change stops voting.

Cadence without exhausting people

Community posts appear in subscriber feeds, which means over-posting competes with your own videos for attention and irritates people into unsubscribing.

Two or three posts a week is a reasonable ceiling for most channels. One a week is entirely sufficient. The rhythm that works well for a fortnightly upload schedule: a poll early in the production cycle to shape the video, a work-in-progress image midway, and a genuine post at publication that says something rather than just linking.

Consistency beats volume here as everywhere else. A channel that posts once a week reliably outperforms one that posts six times and then vanishes for a month.

Supporting an upload properly

The announcement post is where most creators waste the tool. "New video out now" plus a thumbnail gives a viewer no more reason to click than the video already sitting in their subscriptions feed.

What works better is giving the post its own value:

  1. Lead with the interesting finding, not the fact that a video exists. "It turns out the thing everyone recommends is wrong for most people — full explanation in the new video" is a reason.
  2. Ask something related. A question invites a reply, and replies signal engagement.
  3. Post a still that raises a question the video answers, rather than the thumbnail people have already seen.
  4. Follow up days later. Most subscribers never saw the first post. A second angle a few days on reaches different people.
  5. Resurface old videos in context. When something in the news relates to a video you made two years ago, that is a free, relevant post.

Knowing whether it is working

Community posts have their own analytics, and they answer a question worth asking: are these people actually engaged, or are you posting into a void?

The figures to watch are impressions, likes, comments and, for polls, total votes. Compare vote counts against your subscriber number rather than against another channel's. A poll pulling votes from a healthy fraction of your active audience means the tab is reaching people; one pulling a handful means either your posts are not surfacing or they are not worth engaging with.

The more useful signal is directional. If engagement per post is climbing over months while your posting frequency stays flat, the audience is warming. If it is falling, you are posting too often, or posting things that do not merit attention, and the fix is fewer and better rather than more.

Check availability for your channel

Search the YouTube Help Centre for "community posts" to confirm current eligibility for your channel. Access requirements have changed more than once, so check rather than assuming.

What goes wrong

  • Only posting announcements. Trains the audience that your posts contain nothing, so they stop looking.
  • Posting too often. Feed fatigue is real, and the unsubscribe is quiet.
  • Ignoring the replies. A post that generates fifty comments you never answer is worse than no post — you asked and then did not listen.
  • Polls with no consequence. Asking and then doing something else teaches people their vote is decorative.
  • Reposting other platforms verbatim. The context is different and it reads as broadcast rather than conversation.
  • Going silent for months. Sporadic use produces sporadic results; the tab rewards regular presence.

The mental model that works: treat it as the place you talk to people who already like you, rather than a distribution channel for links. That framing produces posts worth reading, and posts worth reading are what make the tab function at all.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I post on the community tab?

Two or three times a week is a reasonable ceiling; once a week is entirely sufficient. Posts appear in subscriber feeds, so over-posting competes with your own videos and prompts unsubscribes.

Do community posts help videos perform better?

Indirectly. They keep you present between uploads, which supports the click when a video does arrive, and a warmed-up audience gives a new video a better start through early engagement.

What should I post other than video announcements?

Polls that shape upcoming videos, work in progress, a short useful tip, a question, or resurfacing an older video when something makes it relevant again. Announcement-only posting trains people that your posts contain nothing.
