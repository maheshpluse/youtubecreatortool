YouTube gives every creator an enormous amount of data about their videos. The problem is not a lack of statistics; it is knowing which ones to look at, what they actually measure, and what to change when a number looks bad. Most creators glance at views, feel good or bad, and move on — missing the patterns that would tell them exactly how to make the next video perform better.

This guide walks through every important YouTube video statistic, explains how each one is calculated, shows where to find it in YouTube Studio, and connects it to a specific decision. It also covers the public statistics you can see on other creators' videos, and how to use them for competitive research without breaking any rules.

Where video statistics live

Open YouTube Studio, select Content, and click the analytics icon next to any video. You will see tabs for Overview, Reach, Engagement, Audience and, if you are in the Partner Program, Revenue. The Advanced mode link in the top-right corner opens a table where you can compare videos, filter by traffic source, geography or device, and export data.

YouTube's own guide to YouTube Analytics describes each report. Data is usually delayed by up to a couple of days, and some figures — especially revenue — are estimates until the month is finalised.

Compare like with like

A video's statistics mean little on their own. Always compare against your own channel's typical performance for similar videos over the same time window after publishing — for example, the first 7 or 28 days. YouTube Studio shows this as a "typical performance" range on many charts.

Reach statistics: impressions and CTR

Impressions

An impression is counted when your thumbnail is shown to a viewer on YouTube for at least one second with at least 50% of it visible. Impressions include thumbnails on the home page, in search results, in suggested videos and in subscription feeds. They do not include external embeds, end screens or some other surfaces. YouTube explains the full definition in its help article on impressions and click-through rate.

What it tells you: how much YouTube is choosing to show your video. Impressions rise when the video earns good viewer responses, and fall when viewers stop clicking or stop watching.

Impressions click-through rate (CTR)

CTR is the percentage of impressions that turned into views. It measures how appealing your title and thumbnail are to the people who see them.

What it tells you: whether your packaging works for the audience YouTube is testing. CTR usually falls as impressions grow, because YouTube shows the video to broader, less interested audiences. A falling CTR alongside rising impressions is normal; a low CTR with low impressions suggests the packaging needs work. Our guide to improving click-through rate covers fixes.

Traffic sources

The Reach tab breaks views down by where they came from: YouTube search, browse features (home and subscriptions), suggested videos, external sources, playlists, channel pages, Shorts feed and more.

What it tells you: how people are finding the video, which tells you what to optimize. Search-driven videos benefit from clear keywords in the title and description. Browse and suggested traffic depend on packaging and on how well the video keeps viewers watching.

Search terms

Click into YouTube search as a traffic source to see the actual queries that led to views. This is some of the most valuable data in Studio: real words your audience uses. Feed them into the keyword tool to find related topics for your next videos.

Engagement statistics: watch time and retention

Views

A view is counted when a viewer intentionally starts watching, under rules YouTube uses to filter out invalid or automated activity. Views are the headline number, but they tell you little on their own about whether a video is good.

Watch time

Total hours viewers spent watching the video. Watch time matters for Partner Program eligibility and reflects both reach and the ability to hold attention.

Average view duration and average percentage viewed

Average view duration is watch time divided by views. Average percentage viewed expresses it as a share of the video's length. A 10-minute video with a 4-minute average view duration has an average percentage viewed of 40%.

What it tells you: whether the video delivers on its promise. Compare these numbers only between videos of similar length. If you are consistently low, read our guide to fixing low average percentage viewed.

Audience retention graph

The retention graph shows, second by second, what percentage of viewers are still watching. It is the most actionable chart in Studio. Look for:

  • The first 30 seconds. A steep drop means the opening did not match what the title and thumbnail promised, or took too long to start.
  • Sudden dips. A sharp fall at one point usually marks a slow section, an off-topic tangent, or a sponsor segment.
  • Spikes. Rises mean viewers rewatched or skipped to that part — a sign of high interest.
  • The end. A gradual slope is normal; a steep drop well before the end often means viewers felt the video was finished.

The key moments for audience retention card in Studio compares your intro and specific segments with other videos of similar length. Use it to spot patterns across your channel.

Likes, comments and shares

Interactions show how strongly viewers responded. They are useful signals but easy to over-interpret. A video with fewer likes but far higher average view duration usually did the job better.

End screen and card clicks

These show how many viewers moved on to another of your videos or playlists. High end-screen click rates keep viewers on your channel longer. Our end screen click-through benchmark puts your numbers in context.

Audience statistics

  • Returning vs new viewers — whether the video grew your audience or served existing fans.
  • Unique viewers — the estimated number of different people who watched.
  • Subscribers gained and lost — which videos convert viewers into subscribers.
  • Geography, age and gender — who is watching, which affects advertising revenue and sponsor fit.
  • When your viewers are on YouTube — a heatmap of when your audience is active, useful for scheduling.
  • Other channels and videos your audience watches — a map of what else your viewers like, which is a strong source of content ideas.

If you are still working out who your audience is, our guide to target market versus target audience shows how to combine these statistics into a clear viewer profile.

Revenue statistics

For monetized channels, the Revenue tab shows estimated revenue, RPM (revenue per 1,000 views, after YouTube's share), and playback-based CPM (what advertisers pay per 1,000 monetized playbacks). RPM is the one to plan with; CPM is always higher and reflects advertiser prices, not your income.

Break revenue down by video, by content type (videos, Shorts, live) and by revenue source. You will usually find that some topics earn much more per view than others. To estimate earnings for a video before you make it, use the YouTube earnings calculator and adjust for your audience's countries with the RPM by country tool.

Public statistics on any video

On any public video you can see the view count, like count (unless hidden), comment count, upload date, title, description and, through tools, the tags. You cannot see another channel's impressions, CTR, watch time, retention or revenue — those are private to the channel owner.

Public statistics are still useful for research:

  • Views relative to channel size. A video with ten times the channel's usual views has a topic or packaging worth studying.
  • Views over time. An old video still near the top of search for a topic suggests evergreen demand.
  • Comment themes. Repeated questions reveal gaps you can fill.
  • Metadata. Our tag extractor shows the tags a video uses, and the SEO analyzer reviews how well its title, description and tags are optimized.

Use public data for inspiration and learning, not for copying. Re-uploading or closely imitating another creator's content can violate copyright and YouTube's reused content policies.

Diagnosing a video in ten minutes

When a video underperforms, work through the funnel in order. The first weak step is usually the cause.

SymptomLikely causeWhat to try
Low impressionsWeak early response, narrow topic, or low search demandCheck keyword demand; improve title/thumbnail; promote to subscribers
Impressions fine, low CTRPackaging doesn't appealTest a new thumbnail and a clearer title
Good CTR, fast early drop-offOpening doesn't match the promiseTighten the first 30 seconds; deliver the promise sooner
Steady mid-video dropPacing or structureCut tangents; add chapters; add pattern interrupts
Good retention, few subscribersNo reason to come backExplain what the channel offers; link a next video

Only change one thing at a time on an existing video, and wait several days before judging the effect. Write down each change and its date so you can match it against the analytics.

Benchmarks and why to distrust them

You will see claims that a "good" CTR is a certain percentage, or that a "good" retention rate is another. Treat these carefully. CTR and retention vary enormously by niche, video length, traffic source and channel size. A 3% CTR can be excellent for a video getting huge home-page impressions, and a 12% CTR can be ordinary for a video getting mostly subscriber traffic.

The most reliable benchmark is your own channel. YouTube Studio's comparisons against your typical performance are more useful than any industry average. Track your medians over time and aim to improve them.

Building a simple statistics routine

  1. 48 hours after publishing: check impressions, CTR and the first 30 seconds of retention. Change the thumbnail if CTR is clearly below your typical range.
  2. After 7 days: review traffic sources and the full retention graph. Note what caused dips.
  3. After 28 days: record final numbers in a spreadsheet: views, CTR, average view duration, subscribers gained, revenue and main traffic source.
  4. Monthly: sort the spreadsheet by each metric and look for patterns in topics, lengths, title styles and thumbnails.

Statistics for Shorts and live streams

Shorts have their own statistics. Instead of CTR, look at viewed vs swiped away, which shows how many people chose to keep watching rather than scrolling past. Average percentage viewed on Shorts can exceed 100% because of loops. Shorts traffic comes mostly from the Shorts feed, so search and suggested data are less relevant. Our guide on improving the Shorts view-to-swipe ratio covers what to change.

Live streams add concurrent viewers, peak concurrent viewers, chat messages and, where enabled, Super Chat revenue. After the stream ends, the replay is analysed like any other video, so its retention graph shows which segments held attention.

Turning statistics into better videos

Statistics are only valuable if they change what you do. After each monthly review, write down three decisions: one topic to make more of, one packaging change to test, and one editing habit to change. Then use the tools to act on them — the video ideas generator for topics, the title generator and thumbnail ideas tool for packaging, and the script generator for structure that holds attention.

Over time, you will stop guessing why a video worked. The numbers will tell you, and your next upload will be built on that evidence.

Common misreadings to avoid

  • Judging too early. Many videos, especially search-driven ones, grow slowly for weeks. A slow first day is not failure.
  • Blaming the algorithm. YouTube's recommendations respond to viewer behaviour. If impressions fall, look at what viewers did first.
  • Chasing a single metric. A clickbait thumbnail might raise CTR but lower retention and satisfaction, hurting the video overall.
  • Ignoring small samples. A video with 200 views has statistics that can swing wildly. Wait for more data before drawing conclusions.
  • Comparing Shorts and long-form directly. They are measured differently and serve different purposes.
