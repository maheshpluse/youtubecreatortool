Two channels of identical size, in the same niche, with the same monthly views, can earn $40 and $4,000 a month from affiliate links respectively. The difference is almost never traffic. It is intent matching, placement and trust — three things that cost nothing to fix.

Here is how creators in the second group actually structure it, and the mistakes that keep everyone else in the first.

Intent is everything

A viewer watching "10 Funniest Editing Fails" and a viewer watching "Best Laptop for Video Editing Under $1,200" are in completely different mental states. The first is being entertained. The second has a browser tab open, a budget in mind, and a decision to make this week.

The second video can outperform the first by a factor of fifty on affiliate revenue while getting a tenth of the views. This single fact explains most of the gap between high and low affiliate earners — and it means the lever to pull is which videos you make, not how aggressively you promote inside them.

The five formats that convert

  1. "Best X for Y" comparisons. The highest-converting format on YouTube. The viewer has already decided to buy and only needs help choosing. Always name a single winner — "it depends" is honest but converts poorly, and a clear recommendation with stated reasoning is both.
  2. Single-product reviews after real use. Genuine long-term reviews at 6 or 12 months convert far better than unboxings, because they answer the question the buyer actually has: will I regret this?
  3. Tutorials where the product is the tool. Extremely effective and entirely non-salesy. Someone learning a technique in a piece of software has a natural reason to want that software.
  4. "My setup" / "what I actually use." Trades on credibility rather than persuasion. Works only if it is true.
  5. Problem-solution videos. "How to fix echo in a small room" naturally leads to acoustic treatment. The purchase is a consequence of the solution, not the point of the video.
The 70/30 split

Roughly 70% of a channel's affiliate revenue typically comes from the 30% of videos with genuine purchase intent. Identify which of your videos those are, and make more of them — rather than adding links to everything.

Where to place links and mentions

In the description: above the fold, in the order you mentioned them, each on its own line with a plain label. Nobody scrolls a wall of twenty links, and a wall depresses click-through on all of them.

In the video: one clear verbal mention at the moment the product becomes relevant — usually right after you have demonstrated why it matters, not at the start before you have earned anything.

Pinned comment: the highest-click-through placement most creators ignore. A pinned comment with the two or three links mentioned, written plainly, consistently outperforms description links because it is visible on mobile without expanding anything.

On-screen: a brief lower-third pointing to the description at the moment of mention. Subtle, effective, and it prevents the "where's the link?" comment.

The trust maths

Every recommendation spends a small amount of credibility. Recommend something good and you earn it back with interest — the viewer returns for the next recommendation. Recommend something bad, or something you clearly have not used, and the loss is permanent and silent. Nobody tells you they stopped trusting you.

Three rules protect the balance:

  • Only recommend what you would recommend unpaid. The test: would you tell a friend to buy this if there were no commission?
  • Say what is wrong with it. Naming a genuine flaw increases conversion, because it signals that the positives are real too.
  • Recommend the cheaper option when the cheaper option is right. Nothing builds long-term affiliate revenue faster than visibly telling people not to overspend.

Disclosure done properly

This is a legal requirement, not a courtesy. The FTC's guidance for social media influencers requires that material connections — including affiliate commissions — be disclosed clearly and conspicuously, in a way viewers cannot miss.

What that means in practice:

  • Say it out loud in the video, near the mention. A line like "links below are affiliate links, so I earn a commission if you buy through them — it costs you nothing extra" takes four seconds.
  • Put it at the top of the description, not at the bottom after fifteen links.
  • Use YouTube's paid promotion disclosure when a brand has paid or provided free product, which is a separate obligation covered by YouTube's own policies.
  • "#ad" alone, buried in hashtags, is not sufficient for a spoken recommendation.

Beyond compliance, disclosure is good for conversion. Audiences already assume links are affiliated; stating it plainly reads as confidence, and hiding it reads as something to hide.

Read the rules once, properly

The FTC's Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers is short and plain-English, and YouTube's paid product placements policy covers the platform side. Twenty minutes now avoids a serious problem later.

Choosing programmes worth your time

Judge a programme on four things, in this order:

FactorWhat to look for
Cookie window30 days or more. 24-hour windows lose most YouTube-driven sales, because viewers rarely buy immediately.
Commission structureRecurring beats one-off. Software subscriptions paying monthly compound in a way hardware never does.
RelevanceA 4% commission on something your audience genuinely needs beats 40% on something they do not.
Product qualityNon-negotiable. A refund-heavy product costs you the audience as well as the commission.

Software and subscription programmes are generally the strongest fit for creators, because recurring commission turns one good video into an annuity rather than a one-off payment.

Tracking what actually works

Use a unique tracking link or sub-ID per video. Without it you know your total earnings but not which video produced them, which makes it impossible to decide what to make next.

Review quarterly and look at revenue per thousand views by video, not total revenue. A video with 8,000 views and $600 in commission is a far stronger signal than one with 300,000 views and $700 — the first identifies a format worth repeating.

Seven mistakes that cost the most

  • Linking everything. Twenty links means no links, in practice.
  • Promoting before demonstrating value. The mention belongs after the viewer has received something.
  • Recommending untested products. The fastest route to permanent credibility loss.
  • Ignoring the pinned comment. Free, and consistently the best-performing placement.
  • Skipping disclosure. A legal risk and a trust risk simultaneously.
  • Chasing high commission over relevance. Conversion rate matters far more than commission percentage.
  • Never updating old videos. Dead links on an evergreen video are pure lost revenue — audit your top ten performers twice a year.
