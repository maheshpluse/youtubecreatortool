Ask most creators who their target audience is and you get a category: "people interested in fitness," "gamers," "anyone who wants to learn photography." None of those is an audience. They are demographics-shaped guesses, and they produce videos that are aimed at everyone and therefore land with no one.

A real target audience is specific enough that you can predict what that person searched for this week. This article covers how to find yours using data you already have, rather than by inventing a persona in a spreadsheet.

Why "everyone" is a strategy that fails

Broad content loses on both of the signals that determine distribution. Retention suffers, because a video written for beginners and experts simultaneously bores half its viewers and confuses the other half. And click-through suffers, because a title that tries to appeal to everyone contains nothing specific enough for anyone to recognise themselves in.

The counter-intuitive part is that narrowing accelerates growth rather than limiting it. A tightly targeted video produces higher retention and stronger satisfaction signals within its small initial audience — and that is precisely the performance that causes YouTube to widen distribution to a larger one. Broad reach is the output of narrow targeting, not an alternative to it.

The three Studio reports that tell you the truth

1. "Other videos your audience watched." Found in the Audience tab, this is the most valuable audience research on the platform and most creators never open it. It shows what your viewers watch when they are not watching you.

Read it for patterns rather than individual channels. If your cooking audience also watches budgeting and small-apartment content, you are not making a cooking channel — you are making a channel for people managing a constrained life, and food happens to be the entry point. That reframing changes every future topic decision.

2. Traffic sources and search terms. The queries bringing people to your videos are your audience describing themselves in their own words. Note the vocabulary especially: if viewers search "cheap" while you write "affordable," they will find someone else's video.

3. Age, geography and viewing device. Less glamorous but decisive. An audience watching mostly on mobile needs different thumbnail typography and different on-screen text than a desktop audience. An audience concentrated in one country needs examples, prices and references that make sense there.

Where to find these reports

YouTube's guide to understanding your content performance covers the Audience tab, and YouTube for Creators walks through audience research for new channels. Open Studio alongside either one.

Reading the gap between intended and actual audience

Almost every channel has a gap between the audience it is written for and the audience it attracts. The gap is diagnostic, and there are three common shapes.

GapWhat it usually meansWhat to change first
Audience is more beginner than intendedTitles use general language; content assumes knowledgeEither simplify content or use expert vocabulary in titles to filter
Audience is more casual than intendedPackaging is entertainment-shaped, content is instructionalAlign thumbnails with the actual depth of the video
Audience is in unexpected countriesTopic has global demand you did not plan forDecide deliberately: serve them, or re-target with local specifics

The important discipline is deciding rather than drifting. An unexpected audience is not a problem — an unexamined one is.

Building a viewer profile that changes decisions

A useful profile is not a demographic sheet. It is four sentences that make future decisions easier. Write yours as:

  • Situation: where they are right now. "Six months into learning video editing, editing for a small business on the side."
  • Problem: the thing that makes them search. "Their exports look worse than what they see on screen and they cannot work out why."
  • Constraint: what limits their options. "One laptop, no budget for plugins, editing in the evenings."
  • Aspiration: what they want to be true in six months. "Delivering work they are not embarrassed by, and charging for it."

Now the profile does work. It rules topics in and out, it tells you what jargon to explain, it tells you which price bracket to feature in a gear video, and it gives you the vocabulary for titles. A profile that does not change what you make next week is decoration.

The one-person test

If you cannot name an actual person — someone you have met, or a specific commenter — who matches your profile, it is still too abstract. Real creators consistently describe writing for one identifiable person as the change that made their content click.

Testing it before you commit

Do not restructure a whole channel on a hypothesis. Test it across three or four videos.

Publish a small run aimed unambiguously at your candidate audience — using their vocabulary in the title, their constraint in the framing, their level of prior knowledge in the script. Then compare, against your channel baseline: average percentage viewed, comment-to-view ratio, and returning viewers over the following month.

The signal you are looking for is not more views. It is better retention and more comments from people describing themselves. Comments like "this is exactly my situation" are the qualitative confirmation that the targeting landed.

How narrow is too narrow?

The practical test: can you name at least fifty video topics that this audience would genuinely want, and does the audience contain enough people to sustain a channel? "Left-handed classical guitarists in Ireland" fails the second test. "Self-taught developers in their first six months of job hunting" passes both — there are millions of them, and the topic list is effectively endless.

A good target audience is narrow in identity and broad in topic. That combination gives you a recognisable voice and a publishing calendar that never runs dry.

Applying it to titles, thumbnails and scripts

Titles: include the identifier the audience recognises. "For Beginners," "On a Budget," "Without a Studio," "If You Already Know the Basics" — each one is a filter, and filters raise retention by keeping the wrong viewers out.

Thumbnails: show the situation, not just the subject. A tidy professional studio in the thumbnail contradicts a video about filming in a small bedroom, and viewers notice the mismatch in the first ten seconds.

Scripts: write to one person. Say "you" and mean a specific individual. Address the objection that person would actually raise. Skip the disclaimers aimed at people who were never your audience — they add length, cost retention, and protect you from nobody.

Google's guidance for content across its platforms makes the same point from a different angle: content works when it is created for a real audience with a real need, rather than assembled to satisfy a distribution system. Knowing precisely who that audience is turns that principle into something you can act on every single upload.
