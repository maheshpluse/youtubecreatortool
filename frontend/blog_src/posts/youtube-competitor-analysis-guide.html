Competitor analysis on YouTube usually degenerates into imitation. A creator finds a channel doing well, copies its topics, and produces a slightly worse version of a video that already exists — which is the one strategy guaranteed not to work, because YouTube has no reason to recommend the second-best answer to a question already answered.

Useful competitor analysis looks for the opposite thing: what a successful channel is not doing. This is a six-step teardown you can run in an afternoon, once a quarter, that produces a list of specific opportunities rather than a list of topics to copy.

The six steps

  1. Choose the right competitors
  2. Map the catalogue, not the hits
  3. Reverse-engineer the packaging pattern
  4. Find the format gap
  5. Mine the comments for unmet demand
  6. Turn findings into a differentiated angle

Step 1: Choose the right competitors

Analysing MrBeast teaches a 900-subscriber channel nothing actionable. Pick four channels in these specific roles:

  • The peer — roughly your size, same topic. Shows you what is achievable right now.
  • The target — five to ten times your size. Shows you what the next stage looks like.
  • The incumbent — the dominant channel in your niche. Shows you what you must not compete with head-on.
  • The outsider — a channel in an unrelated niche whose format you admire. This is where genuinely original ideas come from, because importing a format across niches is the cheapest form of novelty available.

Four is enough. More and the patterns blur into an averaged mush that produces averaged content.

Step 2: Map the catalogue, not the hits

Everyone looks at a competitor's most popular video. That is the least useful data point, because outliers are often luck, timing or a one-off collaboration.

Instead, sort their uploads by most recent and take the last 20 videos. Record for each: title, rough view count, video length, and format type. Then calculate the median view count of those 20 and mark anything at 2× the median or above as an outlier.

This gives you two genuinely valuable things. The median tells you what the channel reliably gets, which is a realistic ceiling for that niche at that size. And the outliers, viewed against a known baseline, tell you which topics actually over-performed rather than which ones simply happen to be old and have accumulated views.

The trap to avoid

A video with 400,000 views published three years ago and a video with 400,000 views published last month are completely different signals. Always compare within the same recency window.

Step 3: Reverse-engineer the packaging pattern

Open the channel's video grid and look at the thumbnails as a set, the way a viewer sees them. Write down what is consistent: dominant colour, whether faces appear, text length, where the subject sits in the frame.

Then do the same with titles. Most successful channels run three or four repeating title structures — a "how to X without Y," a numbered list, a contrarian claim, a personal-experiment framing. Identify theirs.

The insight is not to copy the pattern. It is to know what the visual environment looks like when your thumbnail appears next to theirs in a suggested column. If everyone in your niche uses dark thumbnails with a shocked face, that is an argument for a clean, bright, typographic thumbnail — the value is contrast, not conformity.

Step 4: Find the format gap

This is the step that produces most of the value. Go through the competitor's last 20 videos and classify each by format:

FormatViewer jobCommon gap
TutorialTeach me to do thisBeginner versions; specific software versions
ReviewShould I buy this?Long-term follow-ups after six months of use
ComparisonWhich of these two?Budget-constrained comparisons
Case studyShow me it workingFailures and what went wrong
ExplainerHelp me understandThe "why," when everyone covers the "what"
Commentary/newsTell me what changedPractical consequences, not just the announcement

Almost every channel over-invests in one or two formats and neglects the rest. A channel doing nothing but tutorials has left every comparison and every long-term review on the table. A channel doing nothing but news commentary has left the entire evergreen tutorial library unclaimed. Those gaps are your first ten videos.

Cross-check with real search behaviour

Pair your teardown with how YouTube search works and Google Trends set to YouTube Search. A format gap only matters if people are actually searching for that format.

Step 5: Mine the comments for unmet demand

Sort comments by newest on the competitor's three best-performing recent videos and read a hundred of them. You are hunting for four specific things:

  • Unanswered questions — a video idea, pre-validated, phrased in the audience's own words.
  • Complaints about the video — "he skipped the part where…" is a direct brief for a better version.
  • Adjacent problems — "this worked but now my export is huge" is the next video in a series.
  • Vocabulary — the actual phrases real people use, which usually differ from industry terminology and make far better titles.

This step alone typically yields eight to fifteen concrete video ideas per session, and unlike keyword tool output, every one of them is attached to a real person who asked.

Step 6: Turn findings into a differentiated angle

Finish the teardown by writing one sentence: "Compared with [competitor], my videos are the ones that ______." The blank must be something a viewer would notice within thirty seconds.

Workable differentiators tend to fall into a few categories: a different depth ("assumes zero prior knowledge" or "for people who already know the basics"), a different constraint ("everything under $200," "no dedicated room"), a different evidence standard ("I used it for six months, not six hours"), a different audience ("for people doing this as a side project, not full time"), or a different pace ("under five minutes, no story").

If you cannot fill in the blank, you do not yet have a channel — you have a copy, and copies compete on production budget, which is a fight a small channel loses.

Where to draw the line

Studying public metadata, formats and comments is ordinary competitive research and entirely legitimate. Two things are not: copying thumbnails, scripts or footage, which is a copyright and reused-content problem that puts monetization at risk; and building your channel identity as a reaction to another creator, which caps you permanently below them.

Run this teardown quarterly, not weekly. Done quarterly, it keeps you aware of how the niche is evolving. Done weekly, it turns you into a follower — always one step behind whatever someone else decided to make.
