A brand emails asking for your engagement rate. You open Studio and discover there is no metric with that name. So you search for the formula and find three different ones, each producing a wildly different number for the same channel.

This is not a small annoyance — it is the number sponsors use to price you. Here is what each definition actually measures, which one to put in a media kit, honest benchmarks by channel size, and how to raise the figure without gaming it.

The three formulas in circulation

Formula 1 — engagement per view (the standard):

(likes + comments) ÷ views × 100

This is what most brands and most influencer platforms mean by engagement rate. It is simple, it uses only public data, and anyone can verify it from the outside — which is exactly why it became the default.

Formula 2 — engagement per subscriber:

(likes + comments + shares) ÷ subscribers × 100

Popular in influencer marketing because it measures how activated your audience is rather than how far a video travelled. It flatters small, loyal channels and punishes large channels with many dormant subscribers. Some brands prefer it precisely for that reason.

Formula 3 — the full engagement definition:

(likes + comments + shares + subscribers gained) ÷ views × 100

The most complete picture, and the hardest to compare against anything, because shares and subscribers gained are private data only you can see. Useful internally, awkward externally.

Always state your formula

A 6% engagement rate means nothing without the definition attached. Writing "6.2% (likes + comments ÷ views, last 90 days, long-form only)" signals competence and prevents a sponsor comparing your honest number against someone else's inflated one.

Which one to use, and when

Use Formula 1 as your headline number for sponsors. It is the industry default, it is externally verifiable, and using anything else invites the suspicion that you picked the formula that flattered you most.

Use Formula 2 as a secondary figure if your channel is small but unusually loyal — it is a legitimate way to show that 8,000 engaged subscribers may be worth more to a brand than 80,000 passive ones.

Use Formula 3 internally, to judge which of your own videos actually built the channel rather than merely collecting views.

The downward trend with size is structural, not a sign of decline. Small channels are watched mostly by people who chose them deliberately; large channels reach a majority of casual, algorithm-delivered viewers who were never going to comment. A 2% rate on a million-view video represents twenty thousand actions — vastly more community activity than 10% of a two-thousand-view video.

Niche matters too. Educational, hobbyist and community-driven topics engage well above average; passive-consumption content such as background music, ambience or compilations engages far below it, and that is not a fault.

Why Shorts must be reported separately

Shorts generate enormous view counts with proportionally far fewer likes and comments — the format encourages swiping, not deliberating. Blend Shorts into a channel-wide engagement rate and you will produce a number that badly understates your long-form performance.

Report two lines: long-form engagement rate, and Shorts engagement rate. Sponsors buying a long-form integration care only about the first, and showing both demonstrates you understand your own analytics.

What engagement actually signals to YouTube

A useful clarification: likes and comments are not a direct ranking lever you can pull. YouTube's recommendation system uses engagement as part of a broader estimate of viewer satisfaction — alongside watch behaviour, dislikes, and direct survey responses.

Practically, that means engagement rate is a diagnostic rather than a target. A video with unusually high comment volume relative to views has struck a nerve, and the correct response is to make more videos like it — not to add "comment below!" to every upload. Manufactured engagement without genuine satisfaction does not survive the system's feedback loop.

Comments deserve particular attention. Writing one takes real effort, which makes comment rate a much stronger indicator of investment than like rate. When a video's comment-to-view ratio spikes, you have found a format worth repeating.

Six ways to genuinely improve it

  1. Ask one specific question, once. "Which of these three would you pick?" outperforms "let me know what you think" by a wide margin, because it requires no effort to have an opinion.
  2. Reply within the first two hours. Early replies visibly signal that commenting is worthwhile, and they lift the total substantially.
  3. Take a position. Neutral, balanced videos are respected and scrolled past. A clear recommendation generates agreement and disagreement, both of which are comments.
  4. Leave one deliberate gap. A question you explicitly do not answer invites the audience to answer it.
  5. Pin a comment that starts the conversation rather than one that promotes something.
  6. Narrow your audience. The most reliable long-term lever. Content aimed precisely at a defined group produces engagement rates that broad content never reaches.

Presenting it in a media kit

Give a sponsor four numbers and one sentence of context: average views in the first 30 days, engagement rate with the formula stated, audience geography split, and the top three audience interests from the Audience tab. Then one line describing who your viewers are as people.

Do not quote lifetime totals or your single best video. Experienced brand managers discount inflated numbers immediately, and creators who present honest, well-labelled figures tend to get better repeat business — the campaign performs roughly as promised, which is what the buyer is actually being judged on internally.
