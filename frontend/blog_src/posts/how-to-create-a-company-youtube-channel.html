For most companies, a YouTube channel is the most underused marketing asset they own. Buyers research products on YouTube, customers look for tutorials there, and job seekers watch company culture videos before applying. Yet many business channels are either empty, a dumping ground for old ads, or tied to one employee's personal Google account — a problem that surfaces the day that person leaves.

This guide shows you how to create a company YouTube channel the right way: on a Brand Account with proper access controls, fully branded and verified, set up with policies and workflows that protect the business, and planned around content that actually drives results. It is written for marketing teams, small business owners and agencies setting up channels for clients.

Why a company channel is worth it

  • Search visibility. YouTube is one of the most used search engines in the world, and videos also appear in Google search results. How-to and product videos can bring in qualified prospects for years.
  • Trust. Seeing the people, product and process behind a company builds confidence that text and images cannot.
  • Customer support at scale. Tutorial videos answer common questions once, for everyone, reducing support tickets.
  • Sales enablement. Sales teams can send prospects demo and case study videos at the right moment.
  • Recruiting. Culture and team videos help candidates decide whether to apply.

Personal channel vs Brand Account

This is the most important decision in the whole setup, and the easiest to get wrong.

A channel created directly on someone's personal Google account can only be managed by signing in as that person. A channel created on a Brand Account can have multiple owners and managers who sign in with their own Google accounts. Google explains Brand Accounts in its help page on managing Brand Accounts.

Use a company-controlled Google account

Make the primary owner a Google account the company controls — ideally tied to your company domain through Google Workspace — rather than an individual employee's personal account. Add at least two trusted owners so access never depends on one person.

Creating the channel step by step

YouTube's help article on creating a YouTube channel has the current steps. The general process is:

  1. Sign in to YouTube with the company-controlled Google account.
  2. Open your account menu and go to Settings, then choose the option to create a new channel (this creates a channel with a Brand Account).
  3. Enter your company name as the channel name and confirm.
  4. Choose a handle that matches your company name as closely as possible. Handles are unique, 3–30 characters, and appear in your channel URL. If you need ideas for variations, our channel name generator can help.
  5. Verify the account with a phone number. Verification unlocks longer uploads, custom thumbnails and live streaming, as described in YouTube's feature eligibility page.
  6. Turn on two-step verification for every account with access.

If your company already has a channel on a personal account, check the help pages for moving a channel to a Brand Account before building anything new, so you keep existing videos and subscribers.

Permissions and access control

YouTube Studio has its own channel permission roles. The main ones, as described in YouTube's help page on channel permissions, include:

  • Owner — full access, including managing permissions and deleting the channel.
  • Manager — can edit everything and invite or remove editors and viewers, but cannot delete the channel.
  • Editor — can edit channel content and settings, with limited revenue access.
  • Viewer — can view but not edit. Useful for executives and stakeholders.
  • Additional limited roles exist for specific tasks such as subtitles.

Follow the principle of least privilege: give each person the lowest role they need. Agencies and freelancers should normally be editors, not owners. Review permissions every quarter and immediately when anyone leaves the company or a contract ends.

Branding and channel setup

In YouTube Studio, open Customization.

Profile picture

Use your logo mark, not the full wordmark, centred with padding so it survives the circular crop and stays legible at small sizes.

Banner

Include a one-line value proposition and, if relevant, your upload schedule. Keep essential text and logos in the central safe area so they display on mobile, desktop and TV.

Description

Explain in the first two sentences who the channel is for and what they will learn. Include relevant keywords naturally. The description generator can create a first draft for your channel and videos.

Links and contact

Add your website, key product pages and social profiles. Provide a business email for enquiries.

Layout

Set a channel trailer for new visitors and organise the homepage into sections — for example "Getting started," "Product tutorials," "Customer stories." Our guide to optimizing your channel homepage covers layout in detail.

Upload defaults

Under Settings, set default description text (links, social profiles), category, language and visibility. This saves time and keeps every upload consistent.

Policies and legal essentials

A company channel carries legal and reputational responsibility. Before publishing, set clear rules for:

  • Music and footage rights. Only use music, footage and images you own or are licensed to use on YouTube. Use YouTube's Audio Library or properly licensed stock. Unlicensed music can trigger Content ID claims or copyright strikes. Our copyright and fair use guide explains the basics.
  • Consent and releases. Get written consent from employees and customers who appear on camera.
  • Advertising disclosures. Paid partnerships and influencer collaborations must be disclosed. YouTube has a paid promotion setting, and regulators such as the US Federal Trade Commission publish disclosure guidance.
  • Claims. Product claims must be accurate and substantiated, as in any advertising.
  • Regulated industries. Finance, healthcare and legal businesses often need compliance review before publishing.
  • Comments moderation. Decide who monitors comments, how quickly to respond, and what to hide or report. YouTube Studio lets you add blocked words and hold potentially inappropriate comments for review.
  • Privacy. Never show customer data, internal dashboards or confidential information on screen.

A content strategy that works for businesses

The most common mistake is treating YouTube as a place to upload adverts. Viewers come to YouTube to learn, solve problems and be entertained. Business channels grow when they help first and sell second.

Cadence

Consistency beats volume. One genuinely useful video every week or two, sustained for a year, is far more valuable than a burst of ten videos followed by silence. Our content calendar guide helps you plan.

Measuring business results

Views are not the goal for a company channel; business outcomes are. Track:

  • Search traffic and search terms in YouTube Analytics — are you being found for the problems you solve?
  • Watch time on tutorial videos — are customers using them?
  • Clicks to your website — use UTM parameters on description links so your web analytics attributes visits to specific videos.
  • Leads and sign-ups attributed to YouTube in your CRM.
  • Support ticket volume on topics covered by videos.
  • Sales team usage — how often reps share videos with prospects.

Review monthly. Double down on the topics and formats that bring in qualified visitors, and retire the ones that do not.

Launch checklist

  1. Channel created on a Brand Account owned by a company-controlled Google account.
  2. At least two owners; everyone else on the lowest role they need; two-step verification on all accounts.
  3. Handle claimed and matched to the company name; account verified.
  4. Profile picture, banner, description, links and contact email set.
  5. Upload defaults configured.
  6. Music, footage, consent, disclosure and comment moderation policies written and shared.
  7. First 10 video topics chosen from real customer questions and validated for search demand.
  8. Channel trailer and at least three videos published before promoting the channel.
  9. Tracking set up: UTM links, CRM source, monthly reporting.

Common mistakes company channels make

  • Creating the channel on an employee's personal account. Fixable, but painful. Start on a Brand Account.
  • Uploading only ads. Adverts rarely rank in search or earn recommendations.
  • Vague titles. "Q3 Webinar" helps no one. "How to Reduce Invoice Processing Time by Half" does.
  • Inconsistent publishing. Channels that go quiet for months lose momentum.
  • No owner inside the company. Someone must be responsible for the channel's performance, even if production is outsourced.

Working with agencies and freelancers

Many businesses outsource video production. That works well if the company keeps ownership and control. Put these rules in every contract:

  • The company owns the channel. The agency receives an editor or manager role through channel permissions, never ownership or login details.
  • The company owns the footage. Raw files, project files, thumbnails and scripts are delivered to company storage, so you can change suppliers without losing assets.
  • Licences are in the company's name or clearly transferable, including music, stock footage and fonts.
  • Approval workflow. Videos are uploaded as private or unlisted for review, then published by the company or after written approval.
  • Reporting. The agency reports monthly on agreed business metrics, not just views.
  • Offboarding. When the contract ends, access is removed on the same day.

Repurposing existing company content

Most companies already have material that can become videos: webinar recordings, conference talks, training sessions, sales demos and blog posts. These are valuable, but rarely ready to upload as they are. Cut long recordings into focused videos that each answer one question. Give each a specific, searchable title and a custom thumbnail. Remove internal references and anything confidential. Add chapters so viewers can jump to the part they need.

The reverse works too. A strong tutorial video can become a blog post, a help-centre article, a social clip and a sales email. Our guide to repurposing YouTube videos into blog posts shows the process. Planning content that serves several channels from one recording is how small marketing teams keep a consistent publishing schedule without burning out.

Should a company channel be monetized?

Businesses can apply to the YouTube Partner Program if they meet the eligibility requirements, but ad revenue is rarely the point of a company channel. Ads may show competitors' products next to your videos, and the revenue is usually small compared with the value of a single new customer. Most companies are better served by focusing on leads, sales and support outcomes.
