Whether you are curious how much a favourite creator makes, planning your own channel's income, evaluating a sponsorship offer, or considering buying a channel, you eventually want an answer to the same question: how much does this YouTube channel earn?

The honest answer is that nobody outside the channel knows for certain. YouTube does not publish creator earnings, and any website that shows one exact number is estimating. But you can build a realistic estimate — a range that is useful for decisions — if you understand how channel income is made up and which data you need. This guide explains exactly how, for both your own channel and other people's.

The core formula

For YouTube-paid revenue, everything comes down to one relationship:

YouTube earnings = (views ÷ 1,000) × RPM

RPM (revenue per mille) is the revenue a creator receives per 1,000 views, after YouTube's share, across all views including those without ads. It is defined in YouTube's help article on revenue metrics. For long-form videos, creators receive 55% of net ad revenue; for Shorts, they receive 45% of their allocated share of the Shorts pool, as set out in the YouTube partner earnings overview.

So estimating earnings comes down to two numbers: how many monetizable views the channel gets, and what RPM is realistic for that channel. Views are often public; RPM is the hard part.

Estimating your own channel's earnings

If you are monetized

You do not need to estimate — YouTube Studio tells you. Open Analytics and choose the Revenue tab. You will see estimated revenue, RPM, and a breakdown by source and by video. For forecasting, use at least 90 days of data and look at the last 12 months to see seasonality.

To project future earnings:

  1. Take your average monthly views over the last three months, separately for long-form and Shorts.
  2. Multiply each by its own RPM from the same period.
  3. Adjust for the season: expect higher RPMs in the fourth quarter and lower in January.
  4. Add a growth assumption based on your actual view trend, not hopes.

If you are not yet monetized

Use your real view counts from Studio and an estimated RPM for your niche and audience. Our free YouTube earnings calculator does this for you: enter your views and niche, and it returns a realistic earnings range. Adjust for your audience's countries with the RPM by country tool. Estimating someone else's channel earnings

For another channel, you are working with public data only. Here is a step-by-step method.

Step 1: Estimate monthly views

Look at the channel's videos from the last 30–60 days. Add up the views on recent uploads, then account for older videos that keep earning — search-driven channels often get most views from their back catalogue. A rough approach is to note the channel's total view count today and again in 30 days; the difference is the monthly total. Separate Shorts from long-form, because they earn very differently.

Step 2: Identify the niche

Finance, business, software and technology channels typically have higher RPMs than gaming, entertainment and music. Our average CPM guide shows typical ranges by niche.

Step 3: Estimate audience geography

You cannot see another channel's analytics, but you can infer: the language used, cultural references, the countries named in comments, the time of day the channel publishes, and the sponsors it features. A channel sponsored by US-only services likely has a large US audience.

Step 4: Check monetization status

Signs a channel is monetized include ads on its videos, a Join button for memberships, and Super Thanks on videos. Very new or small channels may not be monetized at all. Some videos may be limited for advertisers even on monetized channels.

Step 5: Apply an RPM range

Choose a low, middle and high RPM for the niche and audience. Calculate earnings for each. For Shorts, use a much lower RPM than long-form.

Step 6: Add visible non-ad income

Count sponsored integrations per month and estimate a fee per integration (see below). Note affiliate links and products. These estimates will be rough, so express them as a wide range.

The earnings calculator handles steps 5 and part of 6 quickly, and the SEO analyzer helps you see how well the channel's top videos are optimized — a clue to how durable their search traffic is.

Always use ranges

An estimate like "$2,000–$6,000 per month from ads" is honest and useful. A figure like "$3,847 per month" implies precision that no outside estimate has.

Worked examples

These examples illustrate the method. The RPMs are assumptions for the example, not facts about any real channel.

Example 1: Personal finance channel

1.2 million long-form views per month, mostly US and UK audience. RPM range assumed: $8–$18.

  • Low: 1,200 × $8 = $9,600
  • High: 1,200 × $18 = $21,600
  • Plus two sponsorships per month and affiliate links — potentially significant additional income.

Example 2: Gaming channel

3 million long-form views and 10 million Shorts views per month, global audience. Long-form RPM range assumed: $1.50–$4. Shorts RPM assumed: $0.03–$0.08.

  • Long-form: 3,000 × $1.50–$4 = $4,500–$12,000
  • Shorts: 10,000 × $0.03–$0.08 = $300–$800
  • Plus memberships and Super Chat from live streams.

Example 3: Small education channel

80,000 long-form views per month, mixed audience. RPM range assumed: $3–$7.

  • 80 × $3–$7 = $240–$560 per month from YouTube.
  • A single affiliate or course sale per day could exceed this.

Notice how the gaming channel has more than ten times the views of the finance channel's long-form content, yet a similar or lower ad income. Niche and audience matter as much as views.

Why online estimates are often wrong

Channel earnings websites typically multiply public views by a very wide fixed range — often something like $0.25 to $4 per 1,000 views for every channel. That produces ranges that are wrong in both directions:

  • They ignore niche. A finance channel can earn many times the top of such a range.
  • They ignore geography. Audience location can change RPM by a factor of five or more.
  • They mix Shorts and long-form, even though Shorts earn far less per view.
  • They count non-monetized views — views from before monetization, from limited-ads videos, or from videos with copyright claims where revenue goes to the rights holder.
  • They ignore non-ad income, often the largest part for mid-sized creators.
  • They ignore costs. Revenue is not profit. Editors, equipment, software, travel and taxes can take a large share.

Income beyond ads

Sponsorships

Sponsored integrations are often priced per 1,000 expected views, adjusted for niche, deliverables, usage rights and exclusivity. For many mid-sized channels, one sponsorship can equal or exceed a month of ad revenue. Affiliate income

Growing your channel earnings

Once you have a realistic estimate, the question becomes how to raise it. The biggest levers are:

  1. More views on evergreen topics. Find topics with steady search demand using the keyword tool and plan them with the video ideas generator.
  2. Higher RPM topics. Within your niche, cover subjects with commercial intent — comparisons, reviews, how-to-choose guides.
  3. Better packaging. A higher click-through rate multiplies every other effort. Use the title generator and thumbnail ideas tool.
  4. Longer watch time. Well-structured videos over eight minutes can include mid-roll ads. Outline with the script generator.
  5. Diversified income. Add affiliate links, sponsorships and memberships so your income does not depend on ad rates alone.

Our guide to increasing your YouTube RPM covers the ad side in more detail.

A note on costs and taxes

Earnings estimates describe revenue, not take-home pay. Full-time creators often spend a significant share of revenue on editors, thumbnail designers, equipment, software, music licences and travel. Taxes apply to creator income in most countries, and creators outside the US may have US tax withheld on earnings from US viewers unless they submit the right tax information. Our YouTube taxes guide covers the basics; a qualified tax professional should advise on your situation.

Summary

  • YouTube earnings = views ÷ 1,000 × RPM, calculated separately for long-form and Shorts.
  • RPM depends mainly on niche, audience geography and season.
  • For your own channel, use Studio's Revenue tab; for others, use public views and a realistic RPM range.
  • Always give a range, never a single figure.
  • Non-ad income is often the largest part of a creator's earnings.
  • Revenue is not profit — account for costs and taxes.

Frequently asked questions about channel earnings

How much does a channel with 100,000 subscribers make?

Subscriber count alone does not determine earnings. A channel with 100,000 subscribers might get 50,000 or 2 million views per month depending on how active its audience is, and its RPM could be $1 or $20 depending on niche and geography. Look at recent monthly views instead.

Do channels earn money from old videos?

Yes. Monetized videos continue earning as long as they get views and remain eligible for ads. Evergreen videos that rank in search can earn for years, which is why a strong back catalogue is so valuable.

Why did a channel's earnings change so much month to month?

Seasonal ad demand, a viral video, changes in audience geography, or videos becoming limited for advertisers can all cause large swings. December and January often show the biggest difference.

Can I see exactly how much another YouTuber earns?

No. Only the channel owner can see revenue in YouTube Studio. Some creators share their earnings publicly, which can be a useful reference, but remember that they may be sharing a particularly good or bad month, and their niche and audience may differ from yours.

Is it better to estimate using CPM or RPM?

Always RPM. CPM is what advertisers pay per 1,000 ad impressions before YouTube's share, and it only counts monetized playbacks. RPM is what the creator actually receives per 1,000 views. Using CPM will overestimate earnings, often by two to three times.

Building an earnings forecast for your own channel

If you are planning to go full-time or invest in your channel, build a simple 12-month forecast in a spreadsheet. Create one row per month with columns for expected long-form views, Shorts views, long-form RPM, Shorts RPM, sponsorship income, affiliate income and costs. Base each figure on your own last 90 days, apply seasonal adjustments (higher in the fourth quarter, lower in January), and add three scenarios: conservative, expected and optimistic. Update it monthly with actual figures. Over time you will see which assumptions were too hopeful and which income sources grow most reliably, and your decisions will be based on evidence rather than a single good month.
