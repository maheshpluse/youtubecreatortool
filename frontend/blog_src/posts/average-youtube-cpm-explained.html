"What is the average CPM on YouTube?" is one of the most searched monetization questions, and one of the most misunderstood. A single platform-wide average hides enormous variation — by country, niche, season, video format and audience — and it describes what advertisers pay, not what creators receive. If you plan your channel income around an average CPM you read in a forum, you will almost certainly overestimate.

This guide explains exactly what YouTube CPM measures, the realistic ranges you can expect across niches and countries, how CPM differs from RPM, why CPM changes through the year, and the specific levers that raise it. It relies on YouTube's own definitions, linked throughout, and gives you a method to estimate your channel's numbers rather than borrowing someone else's.

What CPM means on YouTube

CPM stands for cost per mille — the cost per thousand. In YouTube Studio you will see two related metrics, both defined in YouTube's help article on revenue metrics:

  • CPM — how much advertisers pay for every 1,000 ad impressions on your content.
  • Playback-based CPM — the estimated gross revenue per 1,000 playbacks where at least one ad was shown.

Both are measured before YouTube's revenue share and only count monetized impressions or playbacks. That is why CPM is always higher than the amount you earn per 1,000 views.

CPM is an advertiser metric

CPM describes the price of advertising on your videos. It is useful for understanding demand for your audience, but it is not your income. For income, use RPM.

So what is the average CPM?

YouTube does not publish a platform-wide average CPM, and any single figure you see is an estimate drawn from creator reports, advertising platforms or industry surveys. Across those sources, long-form YouTube CPMs commonly fall somewhere between about $2 and $12 for many channels, with some niches and audiences reaching well above $20 and others falling below $1.

That range is so wide because CPM is set by an auction. Advertisers bid to reach specific viewers in specific contexts, and the price depends on how many advertisers want that viewer and how much a customer is worth to them. A viewer in a high-income market watching a video about business software is worth far more to advertisers than a viewer in a lower-spend market watching a comedy clip.

So the useful question is not "what is the average CPM?" but "what is a typical CPM for a channel like mine?"

Average CPM by country

Audience location is the other dominant factor. Advertisers spend more per viewer in countries with larger advertising markets and higher consumer spending. Channels with audiences mainly in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, Germany, the Nordic countries and similar markets typically see CPMs several times higher than channels with audiences mainly in lower-spend ad markets.

This has a practical consequence: two channels in the same niche with the same views can earn very different amounts. It also means language and cultural choices — which audiences your content serves — directly affect CPM.

How CPM changes through the year

Advertising budgets follow the calendar, and CPM follows the budgets:

  • January — usually the lowest point of the year. Budgets reset and many advertisers pause after the holidays.
  • February to March — gradual recovery. The end of the first quarter can bring a small rise.
  • April to August — relatively stable, with local events influencing specific niches.
  • September — back-to-school and autumn campaigns lift CPMs in education, technology and fashion.
  • October to December — the strongest period. Holiday shopping, major sale events and year-end budget spending often push CPMs to their annual high.

Some niches have their own seasons: tax topics peak before filing deadlines, fitness in January (despite low overall CPMs), travel before summer. Plan high-value topics to publish before these peaks. Our guide to building a content calendar shows how.

CPM vs RPM: the number you actually earn

RPM (revenue per mille) is your total revenue per 1,000 views, after YouTube's share, across all views including those with no ads. RPM includes other revenue sources too, such as YouTube Premium, channel memberships and Super Chat.

Here is how the gap forms. Suppose a video gets 100,000 views and 50,000 of them are monetized playbacks. The playback-based CPM is $10, so advertisers spend around $500. Under the YouTube partner earnings terms, creators receive 55% of net ad revenue on long-form video, so you earn about $275. Your RPM is $275 ÷ 100,000 × 1,000 = $2.75 — far below the $10 CPM.

For planning, always use RPM. Put your views and niche into the YouTube earnings calculator to see an earnings range based on realistic RPMs.

What about Shorts CPM?

Shorts are monetized through a revenue-sharing pool rather than ads attached to individual videos, as explained in YouTube's Shorts monetization policies. Ads run between Shorts in the feed, the revenue is pooled, part covers music licensing, and eligible creators receive 45% of their allocated share. Because of this, Shorts RPMs are typically far lower than long-form, and traditional CPM comparisons do not apply cleanly. Our Shorts monetization guide covers the details.

How to raise your CPM

You cannot set your CPM, but you can make your content more valuable to advertisers.

1. Cover topics with commercial intent

Videos that help viewers choose, buy or use products and services attract higher-value advertisers. Within your niche, look for "best," "vs," "review," "how to choose," and "how to set up" topics. Use the keyword tool to find which of these have search demand.

2. Keep content advertiser-friendly

Videos that meet YouTube's advertiser-friendly content guidelines can receive the full range of advertisers. Strong profanity, especially early in the video, graphic content, and sensitive topics without context can limit ads. Be accurate when completing the self-certification questionnaire in Studio.

3. Use mid-roll ads wisely on longer videos

Videos of eight minutes or more can include mid-roll ads. More ad opportunities generally mean more revenue per view, although CPM itself may not rise. Place breaks at natural pauses so viewers are less likely to leave.

4. Enable all eligible ad formats

In YouTube Studio you can choose which ad types to allow on your videos. Allowing a broad range generally increases competition for your inventory.

5. Serve audiences advertisers value

Content in widely spoken languages for high-spend markets typically attracts higher CPMs. Adding accurate captions and translated titles can widen your audience. See our guide to multi-language YouTube growth.

6. Publish high-value topics before peak seasons

Get your most commercially valuable videos live before the fourth quarter, so they are ranking by the time budgets peak.

7. Improve retention

Higher retention means more viewers reach mid-roll ads and stay for more ad impressions. Structure videos with a strong hook and clear sections. The script generator can help you outline.

Estimating your own CPM before monetization

If you are not yet in the Partner Program, you can still build a reasonable estimate:

  1. Identify your niche and pick a range from the table above.
  2. Check your audience geography in YouTube Analytics. Adjust the range down if most viewers are in lower-spend markets, up if most are in high-spend markets. The RPM by country tool helps here.
  3. Convert to RPM: as a starting point, take roughly a third to a half of your estimated CPM.
  4. Run the numbers through the earnings calculator with your average monthly views.
  5. Replace estimates with real data as soon as your channel is monetized. After three months, your Studio revenue data is far more reliable than any average.

Common myths about YouTube CPM

  • "Bigger channels get higher CPMs." Channel size itself does not set CPM. Audience, topic and advertiser demand do.
  • "Subscribers see more valuable ads." Ad targeting is based on the viewer and context, not subscription status.
  • "Asking viewers to watch ads raises CPM." Encouraging people to click or watch ads in unnatural ways violates AdSense policies and can get your account disabled. Never do it.
  • "Longer videos always have higher CPMs." Longer videos can have more ads, which can raise revenue per view, but the CPM for each ad is set by the auction.
  • "CPM dropped, so my channel is being punished." CPM changes constantly with advertiser demand. A January drop is seasonal, not a penalty.

Reading your CPM in YouTube Studio

Go to Analytics, then Revenue. You will see RPM on the main card. Click See more or open advanced mode to view CPM and playback-based CPM by video, by country, by device and over time. Useful checks include:

  • Top videos by CPM — which topics attract the most valuable advertisers? Make more of them.
  • CPM by country — confirms how much geography affects your channel.
  • CPM over the last 12 months — shows your personal seasonal pattern.
  • Monetized playbacks as a share of views — if low, check advertiser suitability icons and ad settings.

The bottom line

There is no single average YouTube CPM worth planning around. There is a range for your niche, adjusted for your audience's countries and the time of year, and then converted into RPM — the number that reaches your account. Use the tools to estimate it, replace estimates with your own Studio data as soon as you can, and focus your effort on the things that raise it: valuable topics, advertiser-friendly content, strong retention and publishing ahead of peak seasons.

Ad revenue is only one part of a creator business. Affiliate income, sponsorships and memberships often earn more per view than ads, especially for smaller channels in valuable niches. Our guide to passive income streams for creators covers how to build them alongside AdSense.

Why your CPM differs from video to video

Even inside one channel, CPM can vary by a factor of three or more between videos. The reasons usually include the topic's commercial intent, the audience each video attracted (a video that reached a different country mix will price differently), the time of year it earned most of its views, its advertiser-suitability status, and its length and mid-roll placement. When you review revenue data, sort videos by CPM and look for patterns in topic and audience rather than treating the channel average as a fixed property of your channel.
