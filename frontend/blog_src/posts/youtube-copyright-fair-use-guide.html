Copyright is the one area where a single misunderstanding can end a channel that took years to build. It is also an area where the most confidently repeated advice on YouTube is wrong — "under ten seconds is fine", "I credited the artist", "it is transformative because I talked over it". None of those are rules. Some of them are the exact reasoning that produces strikes.

This is a plain-English explanation of how copyright works on YouTube, what the difference between a claim and a strike really is, and how to reduce risk sensibly. It is educational and general — copyright law varies by country and any actual dispute needs a qualified lawyer.

Claims and strikes are completely different

Almost all creator panic comes from confusing these. They are not degrees of the same thing; they are separate systems with separate consequences.

A channel can carry hundreds of Content ID claims and remain in perfect standing. A channel with three strikes is gone. Treating these as the same thing leads people to panic about the harmless one and misunderstand the dangerous one.

How Content ID actually behaves

Rights holders upload reference files, and YouTube's system scans uploads for matches. A match triggers whatever policy that rights holder has set: monetise it themselves, block it, or simply track it.

Two things creators consistently get wrong here. First, Content ID is a private contractual system, not a court. It reflects what rights holders have asked for, not a legal ruling about your video. Second, it is automated and matches audio aggressively — music playing faintly in the background of a café you filmed in can trigger it.

The routine outcome is that advertising revenue from that video goes to the rights holder instead of you. Irritating, not existential.

What a copyright strike means

A strike is a formal removal request submitted by a copyright owner under legal process. It carries real consequences: the video comes down, features can be restricted, and three active strikes results in the channel being terminated along with any associated channels.

Strikes do expire after a period, provided you complete the required copyright education, but the interim restrictions are genuine and the third strike is final. This is the outcome worth organising your whole content process around avoiding.

Fair use, honestly explained

Fair use is a United States legal doctrine. It is not a permission slip, not a checkbox, and not something you can claim by writing "no copyright infringement intended" in a description. It is a defence, assessed by a court, weighing four factors together:

  1. Purpose and character of the use. Is it transformative — commentary, criticism, parody, education — or is it a substitute for the original? Commercial use weighs against, though it is not decisive.
  2. Nature of the copyrighted work. Using factual material is treated more favourably than using highly creative work.
  3. Amount and substantiality. How much you used, and whether you took the "heart" of it. Thirty seconds of a four-minute song can weigh more heavily than two minutes of a two-hour lecture.
  4. Effect on the market. Does your video substitute for the original or harm its licensing market? Frequently the most influential factor.

No factor decides alone, and there is no duration that is automatically safe. The persistent belief in a "ten second rule" or a "thirty second rule" has no legal basis whatsoever.

The part people skip

Fair use is decided by a court, after a dispute. Relying on it means being willing to defend it. That is a reasonable position for a well-resourced commentary channel and a poor one for a creator who cannot afford a legal argument.

Fair dealing outside the United States

If you are in the UK, Canada, Australia or the EU, fair use does not apply to you. Those countries use fair dealing or specific statutory exceptions, and they are generally narrower.

Fair dealing typically works from a defined list of permitted purposes — criticism, review, news reporting, quotation, parody, research — and a use must genuinely fall within one of them. There is no open-ended balancing test that can stretch to cover a new situation. In practice this means some reaction and compilation formats that American creators treat as normal sit on considerably weaker ground elsewhere.

Myths that get channels removed

  • "Under X seconds is fine." No such rule exists anywhere.
  • "I credited them." Attribution is not permission. Both are required, separately.
  • "It is free promotion." Not your decision to make on their behalf.
  • "I changed the pitch / mirrored the video." Still a derivative work, and detection handles this routinely.
  • "I am not monetising it." Reduces damages, does not create a licence.
  • "It was already on YouTube." Someone else's infringement is not your defence.
  • "I bought the song." Buying a copy grants personal listening rights, not synchronisation rights for video.

Practical ways to stay safe

  1. Use properly licensed music. YouTube's own Audio Library, or a subscription service where you keep the licence certificates.
  2. Keep a licence record per video. A single spreadsheet of asset, source, licence and date resolves most disputes in minutes rather than weeks.
  3. Film your own B-roll where practical. It removes the entire category of risk.
  4. Mute incidental audio. Background music in public places is a very common claim trigger.
  5. Make commentary genuinely dominant. If the borrowed clip is the main attraction and your contribution is a few reactions, the transformative argument is weak.
  6. Check before you publish, not after. YouTube's checks during upload will flag many issues while changes are still cheap.

Read the official material

YouTube's copyright strike basics and its explanation of fair use are short, accurate and free. They are considerably more reliable than any video explaining what you can "get away with".

If you are claimed or struck

Do not act on emotion, and do not file a counter-notification reflexively — a counter-notification is a legal statement that can escalate to court, and it includes providing your contact details to the claimant.

Work through it in order: identify whether it is a claim or a strike; check whether the claim is actually correct, since automated matches are sometimes wrong; if you hold a licence, dispute with the documentation you already keep; if the claim is valid, consider editing the video or replacing the audio, which resolves most cases cleanly. If the amount at stake is significant, or you are considering a counter-notification, take proper legal advice first.

Important

This article is general educational information, not legal advice. Copyright law differs substantially between countries and outcomes depend on specific facts. Consult a qualified lawyer in your jurisdiction for any actual dispute.
