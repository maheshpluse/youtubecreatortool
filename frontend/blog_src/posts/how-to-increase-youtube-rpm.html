Two channels can each get a million views a month and one can earn eight times more than the other. The difference is RPM — revenue per thousand views — and unlike view count, it is a number you can deliberately engineer.

RPM is the honest metric. Google defines revenue per thousand impressions as your estimated earnings divided by the number of views or impressions received, multiplied by 1,000. On YouTube, your RPM covers all views, including the ones that never showed an ad, and it sits after YouTube's revenue share. CPM flatters; RPM is what lands in your account.

Below are eleven levers, ordered roughly by how much they realistically move the number for a typical channel.

The eleven levers

  1. Topic-level advertiser demand
  2. Audience geography
  3. Video length and mid-roll eligibility
  4. Mid-roll placement quality
  5. Advertiser-friendliness of the content itself
  6. Long-form versus Shorts mix
  7. Seasonality and timing
  8. Watch time per view
  9. Audience age and intent
  10. Premium watch time
  11. Revenue outside ads

1. Topic-level advertiser demand

This is the largest single factor and it is set before you press record. Advertisers bid on audiences, and some audiences are worth vastly more per person. A viewer researching business insurance is minutes from a decision worth thousands to an advertiser; a viewer watching a gameplay montage is not in a buying mindset at all.

You do not need to abandon your niche to exploit this. Almost every niche contains high-value sub-topics. A fitness channel has "best home gym equipment"; a tech channel has "project management software compared"; a travel channel has "travel insurance for long trips." Publishing a handful of these each quarter shifts a channel's blended RPM without changing its identity.

2. Audience geography

Ad rates vary enormously by country because advertiser competition varies by market. The same video shown to a viewer in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia or Germany typically earns several times what it earns from markets with less advertiser spend.

This is not a reason to dismiss any audience — it is a reason to know your mix. Open the Audience tab, look at your top geographies, and if you are publishing in English while 80% of views come from low-CPM markets, that fully explains an RPM that feels too low. Topic choice, publish time relative to the target time zone, and language of your packaging all nudge geography over time.

3. Video length and mid-roll eligibility

Videos long enough to carry mid-roll ads can serve several ad impressions per view instead of one. This is the most direct mechanical lever available: more ad slots per view means higher RPM, all else equal.

The caveat that matters

Padding a five-minute idea out to clear the mid-roll threshold usually backfires. The retention loss reduces total watch time and the recommendation system responds accordingly. Add length only when you have more to say — restructure toward formats that genuinely justify it, such as case studies or multi-part comparisons.

4. Mid-roll placement quality

Where mid-rolls land changes how many viewers actually sit through them. Automatic placement is convenient but occasionally lands an ad mid-sentence, and viewers abandon. Manual placement at natural breaks — chapter transitions, a beat after a conclusion, before a section change — keeps more viewers through the break and improves the completed-impression rate.

The practical routine: after upload, open the monetization tab, review suggested slots, and move any that fall inside a sentence or a demonstration.

5. Advertiser-friendliness of the content itself

Limited or no ads status is the fastest way to destroy RPM, and it is often triggered by things creators do not think twice about: strong profanity in the first stretch of the video, detailed discussion of violence or tragedy, or controversial framing in the title. The rules are published in YouTube's advertiser-friendly content guidelines, and the self-certification questionnaire at upload exists so you can flag context yourself.

Two habits pay for themselves: keep the opening minute clean, and self-certify honestly. Inaccurate self-certification erodes trust in your ratings over time, which affects future uploads.

6. Long-form versus Shorts mix

Shorts monetize through a Creator Pool at a fundamentally lower per-view rate than watch page ads. A channel that shifts heavily toward Shorts will see view counts rise and RPM fall — that is expected behaviour, not a bug.

The productive framing is that Shorts buy attention cheaply and long-form converts it into revenue. Track them as separate lines in your reporting, and judge Shorts on subscriber and long-form-view lift rather than on their own RPM.

Watch: how partner earnings are calculated

YouTube's partner earnings overview sets out the revenue splits, and the YouTube for Creators channel covers monetization changes as they ship. Check both before trusting any RPM figure you see quoted online.

7. Seasonality and timing

Advertiser budgets are seasonal and the swing is large. The fourth quarter — particularly the run to the winter holidays — brings the highest rates of the year, and January is reliably the worst month as budgets reset. The difference between December and January RPM on the same channel can approach a factor of two.

Use this deliberately: schedule your highest-value, most commercially relevant videos for Q4, and treat January as the month for experiments, back-catalogue optimisation and content that was never going to monetize well anyway.

8. Watch time per view

Even in a video with fixed ad slots, a viewer who watches to the end has an opportunity to see more ads than one who leaves at 30%. Retention improvements therefore show up twice: once in distribution, and once in RPM. This is the rare lever that helps both audience growth and revenue with no trade-off.

9. Audience age and intent

Advertisers pay more to reach audiences with purchasing power and purchase intent. Content aimed at professionals, homeowners, business decision-makers or people planning a significant purchase attracts higher bids than content aimed at a very young audience — where, separately, content made for kids carries additional restrictions that reduce ad rates further.

10. Premium watch time

YouTube Premium members see no ads, but a portion of their subscription is distributed to creators according to watch time. A channel with deeply engaged viewers who watch long sessions can earn a surprising share of monthly revenue this way. Premium earnings reward exactly the same behaviour as everything else on this list: making things people watch to the end.

11. Revenue outside ads

The final lever is admitting the ceiling. Ad RPM in most niches has a practical maximum, and creators who break past it do so by adding revenue that has nothing to do with advertisers: memberships, a digital product, affiliate commission, sponsorship. Measured as total revenue per thousand views — the number that actually matters to a creator business — a channel with a $12 product attached routinely outperforms a channel relying on a $6 ad RPM alone.

The order to work in

If you want a sequence: fix advertiser-friendliness first because it is free and immediate, then mid-roll placement, then retention, then deliberately publish into higher-value sub-topics each quarter, and finally build one non-ad revenue stream. Geography and seasonality are worth understanding but are largely outside your control — knowing them mainly stops you from misreading a perfectly normal January.
