Search "how to buy YouTube channels" and you will find marketplaces full of listings promising "monetized channel, 50K subs, instant transfer." Some of those listings are legitimate businesses changing hands. Many are not. The gap between the two is wide enough that a buyer who skips due diligence can lose the full purchase price in a week — and, in the worst cases, inherit a channel that is already on its way to being terminated.

This guide is written for people considering a channel acquisition as a serious business decision. It explains what you are actually buying, how ownership transfers work on YouTube's side, the platform policies you need to read before you pay, how to value a channel with real numbers, and the verification steps that separate a sound deal from a scam. It is deliberately cautious: in most cases, the honest answer is that building your own channel is cheaper and safer than buying one.

What you are really buying

A YouTube channel is not a piece of property in the way a domain name or a website is. You do not buy the audience; the audience subscribed to a person, a format or a topic, and they can leave the moment any of those change. What a purchase actually gives you is a bundle of four things:

  1. A library of videos that continue to earn views from search and suggested traffic. This is the most durable asset, and the one that matters most in any valuation.
  2. A subscriber base whose value depends entirely on whether they still watch. A channel with 200,000 subscribers and 3,000 views per upload has a mostly dormant audience.
  3. Channel history and signals — age, watch history and topical association that help new uploads get tested with viewers faster than on a brand new channel.
  4. Any monetization status, which is conditional and can be reviewed again after a change of ownership.

What you do not get is the creator's personality, their editing skill, their sponsor relationships (unless explicitly included and transferable), or any guarantee that YouTube's systems will treat the channel the same way once its upload pattern changes. Faceless, evergreen, search-driven channels transfer best for exactly this reason: there is no face for the audience to miss.

Platform rules to read before anything else

Before you look at a single listing, read YouTube's Terms of Service, the Community Guidelines and the YouTube Partner Program policies. There are three reasons this comes first.

First, account trading is a grey area at best. YouTube's policies against spam and deceptive practices cover the buying and selling of engagement, and the platform provides no official marketplace or escrow for channel sales. A properly documented business acquisition — a company buying another company's brand account, for example — is a different matter from buying an anonymous account on a forum. You should understand the rules as they apply to your situation, and if the amount is significant, take advice from a lawyer who handles digital asset transactions.

Second, monetization is attached to compliance, not to the channel. YouTube reviews channels in the Partner Program on an ongoing basis. A sudden change in content, upload style or ownership can prompt a new review. If the previous owner was relying on reused or repetitive content, that problem transfers to you along with the channel.

Third, strikes follow the channel. Community Guidelines strikes and copyright strikes are attached to the channel itself. Three active strikes within 90 days can lead to termination. Always ask for evidence of the channel's current strike status and verify it yourself during the handover.

A simple rule

If a seller asks you to pay for a channel whose subscribers, views or engagement came from purchased services, walk away. Artificial engagement violates YouTube's fake engagement policy, and channels built on it can have views removed, lose monetization or be terminated — after you have paid.

How a channel transfer actually works

Legitimate transfers use YouTube's built-in permission system rather than handing over a password. The mechanism most transfers rely on is the Brand Account: a channel attached to a Brand Account can have multiple managers and one primary owner, and primary ownership can be transferred to another person after a waiting period.

The broad sequence looks like this:

  1. The seller confirms the channel is connected to a Brand Account. If it is a personal-account channel, it needs to be moved to a Brand Account first — a step that should happen before any money changes hands.
  2. The seller invites the buyer as an owner or manager through the account's permission settings.
  3. After the waiting period Google applies to new owners, the seller transfers primary ownership to the buyer.
  4. The buyer removes the seller from all roles and reviews every connected service: AdSense association, linked Google Ads accounts, merch shelf, connected apps and any third-party tool permissions.

Google documents these steps in its help pages for managing channel permissions and Brand Accounts. Interfaces change, so follow the current documentation on the day rather than any screenshot from a forum.

AdSense does not transfer. Earnings flow to the AdSense account linked to the channel, and that account belongs to the seller. As the new owner you will link your own AdSense account, which is subject to its own review. Plan for a gap in revenue while this happens, and never agree to leave the seller's AdSense account connected "for now" — that means the seller keeps being paid for your channel.

Valuing a channel with real numbers

Channel sales are usually priced as a multiple of average monthly net profit. Marketplace listings often quote multiples somewhere in the range of 20 to 40 times monthly profit, but that multiple should move a lot depending on risk. The factors below decide where a given channel belongs.

FactorPushes the value upPushes the value down
Traffic sourceMostly YouTube search and evergreen suggestedOne viral video, or mostly Shorts feed
Content typeFaceless, evergreen, documented processPersonality-led, trend-based
Revenue mixAds plus affiliate plus sponsorsSingle income source
TrendStable or growing 12-month viewsDeclining for 6+ months
Audience geographyHigh-RPM countriesLow-RPM countries only
ComplianceNo strikes, clean claims historyStrikes, reused content, claimed music

You can sanity-check any seller's earnings claim before you speak to them. Put the channel's recent monthly views into our YouTube earnings calculator and compare the realistic range with what the listing says. Then use the RPM by country tool to see whether the claimed RPM fits the audience geography. A gaming channel with a mostly South Asian audience claiming a $12 RPM is not impossible, but it is a claim that needs hard evidence.

Work an example. A channel earns $1,500 per month on average over the last twelve months. Costs (a freelance editor and voice artist) run $500 per month. Net profit is $1,000. At a 24x multiple, the asking price would be $24,000. If traffic has been declining for eight months, or if 60% of the views come from one video, a lower multiple is justified — and the correct response to a seller who will not negotiate on that basis is to keep looking.

The due diligence checklist

Screenshots are trivially easy to fake. Every number in a listing should be verified in a live screen share where you direct what gets clicked. Ask the seller to share YouTube Studio and walk through the following while you watch.

Analytics

  • Lifetime and last-365-day views, with the date range changed live on screen.
  • Traffic sources: what percentage comes from YouTube search, browse, suggested, external and Shorts feed. Search-heavy channels are more predictable.
  • Top content: how concentrated the views are. If one video drives most of the traffic, you are buying that video, not a channel.
  • Audience geography and age, which drive RPM and future sponsor interest.
  • Returning versus new viewers, to judge whether subscribers still watch.

Revenue

  • The Revenue tab in Studio for at least twelve months, month by month.
  • AdSense payment history showing actual payments received.
  • Affiliate dashboards and sponsor invoices for any non-ad income, with the question of whether those relationships will continue under new ownership.

Compliance

  • The channel's copyright and Community Guidelines status page, checked live.
  • Any videos with copyright claims, age restrictions or limited-ads icons.
  • How the content is produced: who owns the scripts, footage, music and voiceover. If the seller licensed stock or music under their own account, those licences may not transfer to you.

Run a public-side check too. Our YouTube SEO analyzer lets you review how the channel's top videos are optimized — titles, descriptions and tags — which tells you how much of the traffic is structural (well-optimized evergreen videos) versus lucky.

The scams that catch buyers most often

Most losses come from a small set of repeated tricks. Recognising them is most of the defence.

  • The password handover. The seller gives you login credentials, you pay, and the seller recovers the account through the original recovery phone or email a week later. Only accept transfers through Brand Account primary ownership, and pay only once ownership has fully transferred.
  • Inflated subscribers. A large subscriber count paired with very low views per video, a sudden jump in subscribers in a single month, or comments that are generic and repetitive. These channels often carry fake engagement that YouTube may later remove.
  • Hijacked channels. Some channels listed for sale were stolen from their original owners through phishing. If the original owner recovers the channel through Google support, you lose it with no recourse. Ask for a clear ownership history.
  • Pending demonetization. A seller who has received a Partner Program review warning, or whose revenue has just been suspended, sells before the outcome becomes public.
  • Off-platform payment pressure. Requests for cryptocurrency, gift cards or payment "before the escrow opens." Legitimate sellers accept escrow.

Use a reputable escrow service with a written agreement that describes what is being sold (channel, related social accounts, content files, domain if any), the transfer steps, and what happens if the transfer fails. For larger deals, a lawyer should draft or review this agreement.

The first 90 days after purchase

The most common post-purchase mistake is changing everything at once. The audience and YouTube's recommendation systems both reacted well to the channel as it was. Change slowly.

  1. Week one: secure the account. Remove every previous manager, set up two-step verification, review connected apps, and confirm the recovery details are yours.
  2. Weeks one to four: keep publishing in the existing format and cadence. Your aim is continuity, not improvement.
  3. Months two and three: improve the packaging. Test sharper titles with our title generator and stronger thumbnail concepts from the thumbnail ideas tool on older evergreen videos, then measure the change in impressions click-through rate.
  4. From month three: introduce new topics gradually, using the keyword tool to choose ones adjacent to what already ranks.

Keep a written log of every change and its date. If traffic falls, you need to know what you did in the preceding week.

When building beats buying

Be honest about what the purchase price buys you. For a $20,000 budget you could pay a scriptwriter, editor and voice artist to produce a hundred well-researched videos on a new channel that you own outright, with a clean history and every licence in your own name. That channel starts slower, but it carries none of the ownership, compliance or recovery risk described above.

Buying tends to make sense in narrow circumstances: you already run a business in the same niche, the channel has a long, verified record of search-driven evergreen traffic, the transfer is structured as a proper business acquisition, and you have the budget for legal help. Outside those conditions, the numbers usually favour building.

If you are building, start with the fundamentals: pick a topic with the video ideas generator, research demand with the keyword tool, and follow our SEO checklist for small channels. Growth is slower than a purchase, but every subscriber is one you earned.
